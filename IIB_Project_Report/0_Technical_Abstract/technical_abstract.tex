\chapter*{Technical Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Technical Abstract}

This project investigates whether three-dimensional active acoustic localisation can be performed using a structured neuromorphic model inspired by bat echolocation. The task is to estimate target distance, azimuth, and elevation from the echo produced by a known emitted call. This is relevant to edge sensing because localisation systems often face constraints on computation, latency, memory, and power. Whilst low-power operation is not measured directly in this project, it is a key motivating use of neuromorphic systems. Echolocating bats demonstrate that active acoustic localisation can operate within a constrained biological energy budget, motivating the use of sparse neural processing and specialised parallel pathways as design references. The technical question addressed here is whether these biological structures provide a useful way to design an interpretable spiking neural network (SNN) sensing system. This interpretable design makes failure modes easier to inspect and provides clearer targets for optimisation than a black-box system.

The model developed is a complete simulated echolocation pipeline, implemented solely using software. A frequency-modulated call is emitted into a controlled acoustic environment, and the returning binaural echo is generated with distance-dependent delay, propagation attenuation, interaural timing and level differences, and an elevation-dependent comb-filter spectral cue. The received waveform is processed by an IIR cochlear filterbank and a dynamic leaky integrate-and-fire spike encoder, producing a frequency-organised event-based representation. This representation is then passed through three parallel pathway models. Distance is estimated using echo delay, corollary discharge, onset extraction, and delay coincidence. Azimuth is represented using both interaural time-difference coincidence and interaural level-difference opponent comparison. Elevation is represented using a spectral-template model that compares the observed spectrum with expected comb-filter transfer functions. These pathways are simplified functional abstractions rather than detailed anatomical simulations, but each is tied to a specific acoustic cue and biological computation.

The pathway outputs are represented as one-dimensional neural populations. Direct centre-of-mass readouts, calibrated readouts, and continuous-attractor readouts are compared as population decoders. The continuous attractor neural network (CANN) readouts are interpreted conservatively. They provide stabilising coordinate readouts, but they do not act as universal optimisers. In the clean condition, calibration accounted for most of the elevation improvement, while the CANN provided a smaller additional stabilisation and could slightly worsen a readout when the upstream population was already sharp. Their effect is pathway-dependent, so the final model preserves both the raw pathway populations and the fixed readout estimates to use as effective extracted features for the main trainable model.

The main trainable component is a small SNN fusion stage. It receives raw distance, azimuth, and elevation populations, fixed coordinate readouts, and confidence features such as total population activation and spike-count statistics. Two trainable variants are evaluated: a direct readout from the cached pathway features and a residual readout that learns a correction to the fixed pathway estimate. Additional SNN baselines are trained from less structured inputs, including raw binaural waveforms and cochlear spike rasters. These baselines test whether the hand-designed biologically inspired auditory pathways provide useful feature extraction beyond generic SNN processing of the original signal.

The report is organised into 5 Chapters. Chapter 1 defines the localisation problem and motivates the use of biologically inspired spiking computation. Chapter 2 reviews the acoustic cues, biological pathways, and neuromorphic principles underpinning the model. Chapter 3 describes the acoustic simulator, the three parallel feature-extraction pathways, the SC-inspired readouts, and the trainable residual SNN fusion stage. Chapter 4 evaluates the system under noise-free and adverse acoustic conditions, including comparisons with input-only SNN baselines and analysis of the remaining failure modes. Chapter 5 summarises the conclusions, limitations, and priorities for future work.

Performance is evaluated in the constrained operating region of 0.25--5~m and $\pm45^\circ$ azimuth/elevation. Errors are reported as distance mean absolute error, angular mean absolute error, Euclidean position error, and a dimensionless combined error formed from normalised distance and angular errors. Reported ranges are bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals over a 400-sample held-out test set.

In noise-free conditions, the fixed pathway model already performs functional localisation. The calibrated CANN readout obtained a Euclidean mean absolute error of 0.244~m with 95\% bootstrap interval of [0.204, 0.287] and a dimensionless combined error of 0.046 [0.041, 0.051]. The residual pathway-feature SNN yielded the best noise-free result, reducing these errors to 0.125~m [0.110, 0.140] and 0.022 [0.020, 0.024] respectively, with distance, azimuth, and elevation errors of 0.014~m [0.013, 0.015], 2.62$^\circ$ [2.36, 2.89], and 0.27$^\circ$ [0.25, 0.29] respectively.

Environmental noise exposed the main limitation of the fixed model. When an environmental noise field is added before binaural and elevation filtering, defined as 50~dB below the emitted signal, the calibrated CANN baseline suffered a large elevation failure, as well as reduced accuracy of distance and azimuth. The fixed scalar elevation estimate collapsed under noise, but the wider population representation retained ordered information that the trainable readout could exploit. Its Euclidean error rose to 1.577~m [1.447, 1.707], and combined error to 0.263 [0.246, 0.279]. The residual SNN substantially corrected this failure, reducing Euclidean error to 0.297~m [0.266, 0.332], and combined error to 0.055 [0.051, 0.059]. With both environmental noise and controlled delayed echo copies, the residual SNN remained stable, with Euclidean error 0.310~m [0.277, 0.348] and combined error 0.055 [0.050, 0.060]. In these noisy conditions, feature ablation of test data indicated that the residual SNN depends strongly on raw elevation and distance population features, while CANN scalar readouts contribute less when ablated in isolation.

The input-only SNN baselines support the same interpretation. Raw waveform SNNs performed poorly despite receiving the original acoustic signal. Cochlear-raster SNNs performed substantially better, showing that early auditory preprocessing and targeted spike encoding are useful. However, the best overall balance of errors was obtained when the SNN received the full set of biologically motivated pathway features and learned a residual correction. The project therefore demonstrates a hybrid design principle: fixed biomimetic pathways provide interpretable and useful feature extraction, while a small trainable SNN provides contextual integration and residual correction. This supports the use of bottom-up biologically inspired modelling for spiking neural network design in active localisation problems.

The current system remains a constrained single-object, single-pulse, software-based prototype and proof of concept. It used simplified acoustic cues, functional rather than biophysical neural models, and hand-tuned pathway parameters. By demonstrating that biomimetic pipelines can provide superior feature extraction for SNNs over raw data baselines, this model establishes a computational foundation. This work substantiates the use of biologically inspired modelling as a practical design approach for interpretable neuromorphic sensing, and provides a clear foundation for future low-power hardware deployment and continuous multi-pulse active tracking.
