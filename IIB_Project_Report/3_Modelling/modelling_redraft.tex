\chapter{System Modelling and Implementation} \label{ch:modelling}

This chapter describes the final model used to estimate distance, azimuth, and elevation from a single active acoustic pulse. The aim was not to reproduce bat auditory neurophysiology in full anatomical detail, but to convert selected biological computations into an interpretable neuromorphic localisation pipeline. The final system therefore uses fixed pathway models for auditory feature extraction, followed by a small trainable SNN readout for residual cue integration.

\section{Modelling Methodology}

The modelling process was deliberately modular and iterative. Each stage was developed using the same sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item identify a biological mechanism and its likely computational purpose;
    \item replace the detailed anatomy with the simplest useful mathematical abstraction;
    \item test the abstraction in an isolated mini-model;
    \item integrate it into the relevant localisation pathway; and
    \item measure its failure modes before adding further complexity.
\end{enumerate}

This method is central to the project. The aim is to test whether biological principles provide useful design guidance for an engineered SNN, rather than to reproduce every anatomical detail. The cochlea is therefore treated as a time-domain filterbank and spike encoder; the VCN and nuclei of the lateral lemniscus (NLL) as onset-processing and suppression stages; the IC as a coincidence or cue-mapping stage; the AC and DCN as population-formation stages; and the SC as a coordinate readout. Many alternative mini-models were tested during development. This chapter documents the final integrated abstractions and the design decisions that materially affect their behaviour.

The main trade-off is between biological detail and engineering clarity. Known algorithmic principles are retained where they provide a useful computational role, including timing comparison, opponent excitation-inhibition, spectral-template matching, and attractor-based population stabilisation. More detailed anatomy is simplified where it would introduce poorly constrained parameters without improving the engineering test. A small trainable SNN is then used where full three-dimensional cue interactions are difficult to calibrate by hand.

\section{Final System Overview}

The model receives a target location in spherical coordinates,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} = (r,\theta,\psi),
\end{equation}
where $r$ is distance, $\theta$ is azimuth, and $\psi$ is elevation. These coordinates determine the simulated echo received at the two ears. The echo is converted into a cochlear spike raster (CSR), which is then processed by three pathway models. Each pathway produces a one-dimensional population code and a coordinate estimate. These pathway populations are decoded by simple centre-of-mass readouts, SC-style attractor readouts, and finally a trainable residual fusion network.

\input{3_Modelling/redraft_figures/top_level_system}

Figure~\ref{fig:top_level_system_block} summarises the full computational path from active vocalisation to final coordinate estimate.

The final operating region used for the main experiments is deliberately constrained to $0.25$--$5$~m, $\pm45^\circ$ azimuth, and $\pm45^\circ$ elevation. The lower distance bound avoids call-leakage and near-field edge cases where the echo arrives almost immediately; the upper bound keeps echo delay and attenuation within the tuned short-range echolocation regime. The angular limits keep the simplified binaural and elevation cue mappings within a frontal region where they remain approximately monotonic and avoid rear/side ambiguities. The model also assumes one dominant target and one emitted pulse per localisation trial. These constraints are part of the problem definition rather than incidental experimental details.

The key output of every pathway is not just a scalar coordinate, but a population response over candidate values. This is important because a population code preserves uncertainty, ambiguity, and cue strength. The final trainable readout receives these raw populations, the fixed coordinate estimates, and confidence features, rather than receiving only three scalar outputs.

\section{Common Pathway Pattern}

Although the three localisation pathways use different cues, they follow the same design pattern. The cochlea first converts a received waveform into a frequency-time spike raster. A pathway-specific stage then extracts a useful feature and compares it with a reference: the corollary-discharge copy of the emitted call for distance, the opposite ear for azimuth, or an expected spectral template for elevation. The resulting evidence is organised into a population code over candidate coordinates before an SC-style readout produces a scalar estimate. Figure~\ref{fig:common_pathway_pattern} summarises this shared structure.

\input{3_Modelling/redraft_figures/common_pathway_pattern}

This population-code construction is important. A coincidence detector is not a neuron with a distance value stored inside it. It is a comparison unit associated with one candidate delay. When its two input events arrive close together, their membrane contributions overlap and produce stronger evidence for that candidate. An array of comparison units therefore converts temporal agreement into a response over candidate coordinates. FM channels provide repeated, frequency-specific comparisons of the same target delay, improving noise robustness and retaining spectral structure that is also useful for elevation.

The distance and ITD populations use the magnitude of the peak LIF response above threshold, rather than only a binary spike from one deterministic comparison neuron. This is a compact population-level surrogate. Biologically, each modelled comparison unit can be interpreted as a small ensemble of similarly tuned neurons with heterogeneous thresholds and randomly fluctuating subthreshold membrane potentials. A stronger coincident input would cause a larger fraction of that ensemble to cross threshold and pass spikes downstream. The rectified excess voltage therefore approximates a short-window ensemble spike count or firing rate; it is not intended to imply that an analogue membrane voltage is transmitted directly between nuclei. The model does not explicitly simulate this cellular variability, which is a deliberate simplification.


\section{Acoustic Signal and Environment}

\subsection{Emitted Call}

The emitted call is a Hann-windowed FM chirp, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:modelled_call_redraft}. The final experiments used a downward sweep from $18$~kHz to $2$~kHz over $3$~ms at a sampling rate of $64$~kHz. These frequencies are lower than many real bat calls, but preserve the structure needed for the project: a broadband sweep whose echo can be matched against an internal reference while remaining computationally manageable.

The call is
\begin{equation}
    s(t) = A w(t)\sin\left(2\pi\left[f_0 t + \frac{1}{2}kt^2\right]\right),
\end{equation}
where $A$ is amplitude, $w(t)$ is the Hann window, $f_0$ is the starting frequency, and $k$ is the sweep rate.

\subsection{Echo Simulation}

The acoustic simulator applies the sequence shown in Figure~\ref{fig:monaural_simulation_pipeline}. For a target at distance $r$, the dominant echo delay is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t \approx \frac{2r}{v_{\mathrm{sound}}},
\end{equation}
where $v_{\mathrm{sound}}=343~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. In the binaural case, path length is computed separately for each ear, so azimuth creates a small interaural timing difference.

\input{3_Modelling/redraft_figures/environment_pipeline}

Echo amplitude is attenuated using an inverse-square term,
\begin{equation}
    A_{\mathrm{echo}} = \frac{0.7}{L^2}A_{\mathrm{call}},
\end{equation}
where $L$ is acoustic path length and the constant $0.7$ combines target reflectivity and simplified gain effects. Azimuth is encoded through a smooth head-shadow term,
\begin{equation}
    G_{\mathrm{shadow},i}
    =
    \exp\left(s_{\mathrm{shadow}}\sin\theta\,\sigma_i\right),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_L=1$ and $\sigma_R=-1$.

Elevation is encoded with a simplified pinna model. Figure~\ref{fig:comb_filter_redraft} shows how changing the delayed-copy lag moves the resulting spectral notch. The selected-ear waveform is combined with a delayed copy,
\begin{equation}
    y(t)=x(t)+g x(t-\tau),
\end{equation}
producing the comb-filter magnitude response
\begin{equation}
    |H(f)| = \frac{\sqrt{1+g^2+2g\cos(2\pi f \tau)}}{1+g}.
\end{equation}
Changing $\tau$ moves the first spectral notch and provides an elevation cue. In the final model, the first-notch frequency varies linearly from $6$~kHz at $-45^\circ$ elevation to $16$~kHz at $+45^\circ$, with $\tau(\psi)=1/[2f_1(\psi)]$. The delayed-copy gain is $g=0.99$, producing a deliberately deep and measurable spectral notch.

Three acoustic conditions are used in the final experiments. In the noise-free condition, only the target echo transformations above are applied. In the environmental-noise condition, additive white Gaussian noise is inserted before the binaural head-shadow and elevation-filtering stages:
\begin{equation}
    n_i(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_n^2),
    \qquad
    \sigma_n
    =
    \frac{\operatorname{RMS}(s_{\mathrm{call}})}
    {10^{\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{call}}/20}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{call}}=50$~dB and $i$ indexes ear. Referencing the fixed noise floor to the emitted call means distant attenuated echoes naturally have lower effective SNR. In the delayed-echo condition, the returned signal also contains a simple multipath tail:
\begin{equation}
    x_{\mathrm{return},i}(t)
    =
    x_{\mathrm{echo},i}(t)
    +
    \sum_{m=1}^{M} a_m x_{\mathrm{echo},i}(t-\tau_m)
    +
    n_i(t),
\end{equation}
with $(\tau_m,a_m)=(0.85~\mathrm{ms},0.34)$, $(1.75~\mathrm{ms},0.22)$, $(3.20~\mathrm{ms},0.13)$, and $(5.10~\mathrm{ms},0.07)$. This is deliberately a controlled clutter test rather than a room-acoustics model: every trial uses the same ordered set of delayed copies, and the simulator does not model geometry-dependent reflections, surface materials, or diffuse reverberation.

Figure~\ref{fig:environment_waveform_redraft} shows the resulting time-domain signals and makes the fixed noise floor and delayed-copy structure explicit.

\section{Cochlear Front End}

The cochlea converts each received waveform into a tonotopic spike representation. A detailed biophysical cochlea would require basilar-membrane mechanics and hair-cell adaptation; the model instead uses a time-domain IIR filterbank followed by rectification and LIF spike encoding.

\input{3_Modelling/redraft_figures/cochlea_columnar}

Figure~\ref{fig:cochlea_columnar_transduction} shows this as a parallel filter-and-spike pipeline: the acoustic waveform is distributed across frequency-selective channels, rectified, and encoded as a multi-channel CSR.

For channel $c$ with centre frequency $f_c$, bandwidth $B_c$, and sampling rate $f_s$, the IIR pole radius and angular frequency are
\begin{equation}
    p_c = \exp\left(-\frac{\pi B_c}{f_s}\right),
    \qquad
    \phi_c = \frac{2\pi f_c}{f_s}.
\end{equation}
The channel output is
\begin{equation}
    y_c[t] =
    b_c x[t]
    + 2p_c\cos(\phi_c)y_c[t-1]
    - p_c^2 y_c[t-2].
\end{equation}
This gives a time-domain alternative to an FFT cochleagram and is more consistent with a neuromorphic implementation.

The filter outputs are half-wave rectified,
\begin{equation}
    e_c[t] = \max(y_c[t],0),
\end{equation}
then passed into LIF spike encoders,
\begin{equation}
    v_c[t] = \beta(t)v_c[t-1] + e_c[t],
    \qquad
    S_c[t] = \mathbb{I}[v_c[t]\geq V_{\mathrm{th}}(t)].
\end{equation}
After a spike, the threshold value is subtractively removed from the membrane potential and the result is clipped at zero. This reset allows the encoder to emit further spikes while preventing unbounded voltage accumulation.
The threshold and leak are time-dependent. Early in the trial, the encoder is conservative to suppress call leakage and early noise; later, the threshold is lower and the leak is slower to preserve weaker distant echoes:
\begin{equation}
    V_{\mathrm{th}}(t)
    =
    V_{\mathrm{base}}
    \left(
    V_{\mathrm{floor}}
    +
    (V_{\mathrm{start}}-V_{\mathrm{floor}})
    e^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{th}}}
    \right),
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:schedule_redraft} shows the effect of this adaptive schedule in a distant-target example.
\begin{equation}
    \beta(t)
    =
    \beta_{\mathrm{start}}
    +
    (\beta_{\mathrm{end}}-\beta_{\mathrm{start}})
    \left(1-e^{-t/\tau_{\beta}}\right).
\end{equation}
The final schedule uses a threshold multiplier that falls from $16$ to $2.5$ with $\tau_{\mathrm{th}}=16$~ms, and a leak factor that rises from $\beta_{\mathrm{start}}=0.20$ to $\beta_{\mathrm{end}}=0.60$ with $\tau_{\beta}=24$~ms.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering

    \caption{Adaptive cochlear spike-encoding example at 5~m, comparing a high fixed-threshold LIF encoder with a time-varying threshold/leak encoder. The dotted line marks the 4~kHz VCN passband cutoff.}

\end{figure}

\paragraph{Implementation note.}
The IIR filterbank is used in the final model because it is simple and efficient. The same second-order resonant structure can also be expressed as an excitatory-inhibitory state-space loop, showing how a related computation could be implemented with recurrent neural populations. The full equivalence is not used as a separate modelling result.

\section{Why the Model Preserves the FM Sweep}

An important preliminary result was that robust distance estimation required the sweep structure to be preserved across frequency channels. Table~\ref{tab:sweep_noise_key_result} summarises this result. A single-channel coincidence detector is brittle: random spikes can mimic a plausible delay. In a multi-channel FM sweep, independent noise spikes are unlikely to form the correct cross-frequency timing pattern, so the true echo delay survives as a coherent alignment across channels.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \caption{Key channel-sweep noise-robustness result. Errors are from the preliminary delay-estimation test.}
    \label{tab:sweep_noise_key_result}
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}
        \toprule
        Model & Noise-free MAE & Added-noise MAE \\
        \midrule
        Single-channel LIF coincidence & 0.77~cm & 52.47~cm \\
        Multi-channel FM sweep LIF coincidence & 0.77~cm & 0.77~cm \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

This result motivates the rest of the architecture. The cochlea is not merely a biological decoration; it preserves a tonotopic sweep representation that makes downstream coincidence detection more robust. The distance pathway therefore uses channel-wise onset timing, and the trainable fusion model receives population features rather than a collapsed scalar onset.

\section{Distance Pathway}

The distance pathway estimates range from echo time-of-flight. It compares the received echo against an internal corollary-discharge copy of the emitted sweep. The biological analogue is a population of delay-tuned FM-FM neurons whose activity forms a topographic distance code.

\input{3_Modelling/redraft_figures/distance_flowchart}

Figure~\ref{fig:distance_flowchart} shows the modelled distance pathway. The corollary-discharge copy provides the timing reference, while the echo pathway provides the delayed sensory evidence.

\subsection{VCN/VNLL Onset Processing}

The raw CSR contains precise timing information but also noise and repeated activity. The VCN/VNLL abstraction therefore performs onset cleaning. This represents the computational role of fast broadband-onset cells and timing compensation in parallel brainstem pathways, without attempting to reproduce their full circuitry. Channels below $4$~kHz are masked, then a local frequency-time consensus rule accepts an onset only when neighbouring channels support it:
\begin{equation}
    N_c[t] = \sum_{i=-2}^{2}\sum_{j=-8}^{8} R_{c+i}[t+j],
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    C_c[t] = R_c[t]\mathbb{I}[N_c[t]\geq \Theta_{\mathrm{consensus}}],
\end{equation}
where $R_c[t]$ is the masked CSR and $\Theta_{\mathrm{consensus}}=3$. The first accepted event in each channel is retained as the echo onset. This approximates broadband onset extraction while rejecting isolated noise spikes.

\subsection{Corollary Discharge}

Because the system emits its own call, it can compare the received echo with a corollary discharge: an internal copy of the outgoing sweep. This gives the distance pathway a timing reference without requiring the transmitted call to be recovered from the received waveform. A calibrated latency vector $\ell_c$ accounts for delay introduced by cochlear and onset processing. Using sample indices throughout this subsection gives
\begin{equation}
    t_{\mathrm{cd},c}
    =
    T\frac{f_c-f_{\mathrm{start}}}{f_{\mathrm{end}}-f_{\mathrm{start}}}
    + \ell_c,
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the call duration in samples. For candidate distance $r_k$, the expected echo delay is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_k = f_s\frac{2r_k}{v_{\mathrm{sound}}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{DNLL Suppression}

The DNLL stage adds delayed suppression after the first accepted echo onset. This is a simplified model of a lateral-lemniscus inhibitory pathway: its engineering purpose is to prevent late noise spikes or repeated reflections from dominating a single-target distance population. If $t_{\mathrm{first}}$ is the first accepted onset sample and $N_{\mathrm{call}}$ is the call duration in samples, suppression starts at
\begin{equation}
    t_{\mathrm{suppress}}
    =
    t_{\mathrm{first}}
    +
    N_{\mathrm{call}}
    +
    \operatorname{round}(0.75~\mathrm{ms}\, f_s).
\end{equation}
Accepted onset activity after this point is set to zero. This is intentionally a single-target assumption. A future multi-object model would require decaying or resettable suppression rather than permanent inhibition for the remainder of the trial.

\subsection{IC Coincidence and Sweep Facilitation}

The IC abstraction compares the observed echo sweep with delayed copies of the corollary-discharge sweep. Each comparison unit is associated with one channel $c$ and candidate distance $r_k$:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{c,k}
    =
    \left|
    t_{\mathrm{echo},c}
    -
    (t_{\mathrm{cd},c}+\Delta_k)
    \right|.
\end{equation}
This is evaluated using the closed-form peak response of a two-input leaky integrator:
\begin{equation}
    m_{c,k}
    =
    1+\beta_{\mathrm{IC}}^{\delta_{c,k}}.
\end{equation}
If the two spikes are simultaneous, $\delta_{c,k}=0$ and their contributions sum to $m_{c,k}=2$. If they are separated, the first contribution decays before the second arrives, reducing the peak. Thresholding gives the channel-level evidence
\begin{equation}
    s_{c,k}
    =
    \left[
    m_{c,k}
    -
    V_{\mathrm{th,IC}}
    \right]_+.
\end{equation}
Thus, $s_{c,k}$ retains the magnitude by which the peak LIF response exceeds threshold. Under the population interpretation introduced above, a larger value represents a larger fraction of similarly tuned comparison neurons emitting downstream spikes. An array of these units converts temporal agreement into a population response over candidate distances. The final IC model also softly boosts candidates supported by neighbouring channels in the FM sweep:
\begin{equation}
    F_{c,k}
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(c)|}
    \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(c)}
    \exp
    \left(
    -\frac{
    |t_{\mathrm{echo},j}-(t_{\mathrm{cd},j}+\Delta_k)|
    }{\tau_{\mathrm{facil}}}
    \right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    I_k
    =
    \sum_c s_{c,k}
    \left(1+g_{\mathrm{facil}}F_{c,k}\right),
\end{equation}
where $g_{\mathrm{facil}}=0.45$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{facil}}=8$ samples. This is a soft consistency term rather than a hard gate. It uses the redundancy of the FM sweep to reduce the influence of isolated noise coincidences.
The coincidence surrogate uses $\beta_{\mathrm{IC}}=0.992$ and $V_{\mathrm{th,IC}}=1.45$.

\subsection{AC Population Formation and Sharpening}

The AC abstraction first collapses frequency-specific IC evidence into an organised topographic distance response $I_k$, then sharpens that response with a Mexican-hat interaction,
\begin{equation}
    A = [I + K_{\mathrm{MH}} * I]_+,
\end{equation}
where $K_{\mathrm{MH}}$ combines local excitation with broader inhibition. This stage models the transition from distributed frequency-specific evidence to a readout-ready population over distance. It preserves the dominant evidence while reducing broad neighbouring activity.
The kernel is $K_{\mathrm{MH}}=G_{\sigma_{\mathrm{exc}}}-g_{\mathrm{inh}}G_{\sigma_{\mathrm{inh}}}$, with $\sigma_{\mathrm{exc}}=2$ bins, $\sigma_{\mathrm{inh}}=8$ bins, and $g_{\mathrm{inh}}=0.58$.

\section{Azimuth Pathway}

The azimuth pathway estimates horizontal angle from binaural differences. Two cues are retained: interaural time difference (ITD), implemented as a Jeffress-style coincidence population, and interaural level difference (ILD), implemented as an LSO/MNTB-style opponent comparison. The fixed hand-designed baseline uses the ITD pathway because it is more robust in the final constrained full-3D tests, but both ITD and ILD populations are retained for the trainable fusion network.

\input{3_Modelling/redraft_figures/azimuth_flowchart}

Figure~\ref{fig:azimuth_flowchart} shows how the two azimuth cues are separated into parallel timing and level pathways before being combined at the readout stage.

\subsection{ITD Coincidence}

The ITD branch applies VCN onset extraction separately to the left and right CSRs. For candidate azimuth $\theta_k$, the expected right-minus-left delay is approximated by
\begin{equation}
    \Delta n_k
    =
    -f_s\frac{d_{\mathrm{ear}}\sin\theta_k}{v_{\mathrm{sound}}}.
\end{equation}
The observed channel-wise delay is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta n_c = n_{R,c}-n_{L,c}.
\end{equation}
Each candidate azimuth is scored using the same leaky-coincidence logic:
\begin{equation}
    A_{\mathrm{ITD}}(\theta_k)
    =
    \sum_c
    \left[
    1+\beta_{\mathrm{ITD}}^{|\Delta n_c-\Delta n_k|}
    -
    \vartheta_{\mathrm{ITD}}
    \right]_+.
\end{equation}
As in the distance pathway, the rectified term retains the peak LIF response above threshold. It is interpreted as population evidence: a better timing match produces a larger depolarisation and would cause a larger fraction of a heterogeneous local ensemble to emit downstream spikes. Figure~\ref{fig:itd_mechanics_redraft} shows how a small binaural onset offset becomes a graded population over candidate azimuths.

The neuron whose delayed left and right inputs arrive closest together receives the strongest accumulated coincidence evidence, mapping an interaural delay onto a location in the population.

\subsection{ILD Opponent Comparison}

The ILD branch uses spike-count level codes rather than precise timing. This models the LSO/MNTB opponent computation: same-side excitation is compared with opposite-side inhibition so that relative binaural level survives changes in overall loudness. For each ear and frequency channel, a bank of threshold fractions $\rho_q$ converts spike counts $N_L(c)$ and $N_R(c)$ into level codes:
\begin{equation}
    X_L(c)
    =
    \sum_q
    \mathbb{I}
    \left[
    N_L(c)\geq \rho_q N_{\max}(c)
    \right],
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    X_R(c)
    =
    \sum_q
    \mathbb{I}
    \left[
    N_R(c)\geq \rho_q N_{\max}(c)
    \right],
\end{equation}
where $N_{\max}(c)=\max(N_L(c),N_R(c),1)$. Same-side excitation and opposite-side inhibition are then combined:
\begin{equation}
    L_{\mathrm{left}}(c)
    =
    [w_E X_L(c)-w_I X_R(c)]_+,
    \qquad
    L_{\mathrm{right}}(c)
    =
    [w_E X_R(c)-w_I X_L(c)]_+.
\end{equation}
The opponent balance is
\begin{equation}
    b
    =
    \frac{\sum_c L_{\mathrm{right}}(c)-\sum_c L_{\mathrm{left}}(c)}
    {\sum_c L_{\mathrm{right}}(c)+\sum_c L_{\mathrm{left}}(c)+\epsilon}.
\end{equation}
Candidate azimuths are scored by comparing this balance with the expected monotonic coordinate,
\begin{equation}
    A_{\mathrm{ILD}}(\theta_k)
    =
    D\exp
    \left[
    -
    \frac{(b-\sin\theta_k)^2}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{ILD}}^2}
    \right],
\end{equation}
where $D=\sum_c L_{\mathrm{left}}(c)+\sum_c L_{\mathrm{right}}(c)$ is total opponent drive. The threshold normalisation prioritises relative binaural difference over absolute loudness, while $D$ restores a confidence term so that weak noise responses do not receive the same weight as a strong echo.

The ILD population is useful but not sufficient as a fixed scalar readout. In isolated tests it can be calibrated accurately, but in full 3D it is confounded by range-dependent amplitude and elevation-dependent spectral filtering. It is therefore treated as complementary evidence for the residual fusion network.

\section{Elevation Pathway}

The elevation pathway estimates vertical angle from monaural spectral shaping. Unlike distance and azimuth, elevation is not represented by a single robust timing cue in this model. The simplified pinna therefore creates an elevation-dependent comb-filter response, and the DCN-like population compares the observed selected-ear spectrum with expected transfer templates. This models the use of frequency-specific excitation and inhibition to identify informative spectral notches.

\input{3_Modelling/redraft_figures/elevation_flowchart}

Figure~\ref{fig:elevation_flowchart} shows the resulting monaural elevation pathway, where the selected-ear cochleagram is transformed into a spectral population over candidate elevations.

The selected-ear signal is collapsed into an observed spectral profile $P_{\mathrm{obs}}(f_c)$ over cochlear channels. This profile is equalised against the expected no-notch spectrum:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{P}(f_c)
    =
    \frac{P_{\mathrm{obs}}(f_c)}{P_0(f_c)+\epsilon}.
\end{equation}
For candidate elevation $\psi_k$, the expected comb-filter transfer function is $H_k(f_c)$. The mismatch is
\begin{equation}
    E_k
    =
    \sum_c
    m_k(f_c)
    \left(\tilde{P}(f_c)-H_k(f_c)\right)^2,
\end{equation}
and the DCN population response is
\begin{equation}
    R_k
    =
    \exp\left(-\frac{E_k}{2\sigma^2}\right).
\end{equation}
The final model uses signal-weighted template weights,
\begin{equation}
    m_k(f_c)=P_0(f_c)(1-H_k(f_c))^2.
\end{equation}
This gives high weight to frequencies where the emitted signal has useful energy and the candidate elevation predicts a strong notch.

Figure~\ref{fig:elevation_mechanics_redraft} shows this progression from pinna filtering to a DCN population. The equalisation and signal-weighted template terms reduce sensitivity to the spectrum of the emitted call, but do not remove it completely. In particular, the observed cochlear profile still drops towards the upper end of the frequency range, where the returned signal contains less usable energy than the simplified template assumes. This can bias the elevation population and contributes to the poorer high-elevation behaviour. A more complete model would use a better calibrated emitted-spectrum baseline and a more realistic pinna transfer function.

\section{The SC Model Readout}

Each pathway produces a one-dimensional population code over candidate distance, azimuth, or elevation. The SC model is the coordinate readout stage applied to these populations. Three readout types are used in the project: direct centre of mass, calibrated centre of mass, and an SC-style attractor readout.

\subsection{Direct and Calibrated COM}

The simplest readout is centre of mass:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{q}
    =
    \frac{\sum_k P_k q_k}{\sum_k P_k+\epsilon},
\end{equation}
where $P_k$ is population activity at candidate coordinate $q_k$. This readout gives sub-bin estimates and is used as the direct baseline.

Some populations contain the correct ordering information but have a systematic nonlinear scalar distortion. In those cases, a calibrated readout is applied after COM. The most important example is elevation, where inverse-sigmoid calibration corrects a stable monotonic compression of the population readout. This calibration is treated as a readout mapping, not as a new sensory pathway.

\subsection{SC Attractor Readout}

The CANN is reframed here as one possible SC model readout. It receives the same pathway population as the direct COM readout, but evolves it through a recurrent finite-line population before decoding. Its role is not to create new sensory information; it is to provide a biologically motivated coordinate population that can smooth and stabilise activity. Unlike a direct COM calculation, its recurrent state can persist after an input changes. The readout therefore provides a simple form of memory, creating a natural route to temporal tracking when successive echoes are processed over time.

The implemented CANN is a rate-based population abstraction rather than a fully spiking circuit. This is still compatible with a neuromorphic interpretation because the rates can be viewed as short-window spike counts or average firing activity from an underlying spiking population.

\input{3_Modelling/redraft_figures/cann_topographic}

Figure~\ref{fig:cann_topographic_mapping} shows this topographic readout interpretation: upstream pathway activity drives a recurrent coordinate population, which is then decoded into a scalar estimate.

The continuous-time rate dynamics are
\begin{equation}
    \tau\dot{x} = -x + Wx + Bu,
\end{equation}
where $u$ is the pathway population, $x$ is the SC state, $B$ maps pathway activity into the attractor, and $W$ is the recurrent interaction matrix. The output population is
\begin{equation}
    y=Cx.
\end{equation}
The implemented model uses a two-block excitatory-inhibitory state,
\begin{equation}
    x=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x_E\\
        x_I
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    C=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The final readout is COM over the rectified excitatory population.

Several finite-line input corrections were tested during development, but the important point for the final system is that the attractor is a boundary-aware recurrent readout. Its empirical effect is pathway-dependent: it modestly improves some readouts, worsens or barely changes others, and cannot recover information that is missing from the upstream population. For this reason, the final trainable readout receives both raw pathway populations and CANN readouts.

\section{Trainable Residual Fusion Network}

The fixed pathway model is interpretable, but full 3D localisation introduces cue interactions that are difficult to remove by hand. Distance changes echo amplitude, elevation changes spectral energy used by ILD, and azimuth affects which ear is most informative for elevation. A small trainable SNN was therefore added as a final fusion stage.

\subsection{Feature Vector}

The SNN receives cached pathway features,
\begin{equation}

    \left[
    P_r,\,
    P^{\mathrm{ITD}}_{\theta},\,
    P^{\mathrm{ILD}}_{\theta},\,

    \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{CANN}},\,

\subsection{Residual and Direct Readouts}

Two trainable readout forms were tested. The direct SNN predicts the coordinate target directly from $z$. The residual SNN predicts a correction to the fixed pathway estimate:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_{\mathrm{final}}
    =
    \hat{y}_{\mathrm{pathway}}
    +
    \lambda f_{\mathrm{SNN}}(z).
\end{equation}
The residual form is the preferred model because it preserves the interpretable pathway estimate and asks the SNN only to learn context-dependent corrections.

The target vector is
\begin{equation}
    y=
    \left[
    r_{\mathrm{norm}},
    \sin\theta,
    \cos\theta,
    \sin\psi,
    \cos\psi
    \right]^{\top}.
\end{equation}
Distance is normalised to the constrained operating range, while sine/cosine angle targets avoid angular discontinuities.

\subsection{Training Objective}

The three tasks have different units and scales, so the SNN uses learned uncertainty weighting:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}
    =
    \frac{\mathcal{L}_r}{2\sigma_r^2}+\log\sigma_r
    +
    \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\theta}}{2\sigma_{\theta}^2}+\log\sigma_{\theta}
    +
    \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\psi}}{2\sigma_{\psi}^2}+\log\sigma_{\psi}.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{L}_r$ is distance MSE and $\mathcal{L}_{\theta}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\psi}$ are sine/cosine angular losses. A standard unweighted MSE would require manually choosing the relative importance of range, azimuth, and elevation; uncertainty weighting learns these relative weights during training and reduces the risk that one coordinate dominates purely because of its scale or encoding.

\section{Input-Only SNN Baselines}

The central experimental question is not simply whether an SNN can fit the constrained localisation task. It is whether the fixed, biologically inspired pathway models provide useful feature extraction before the trainable stage. To test this, four input-only SNN baselines remove the cue-specific distance, azimuth, elevation, and SC processing stages. They receive earlier representations of the same simulated binaural waveforms and predict the coordinate target directly.

These controls use the same target encoding, uncertainty-weighted loss, hidden-layer width, simulation duration, optimiser, and train-validation-test split as the proposed fusion network. They therefore test the value of the pathway representation while keeping the downstream training method approximately fixed. Since an input-only baseline has no fixed pathway estimate to correct, it uses a direct SNN readout rather than a residual correction.

\subsection{Baseline Input Representations}

The least structured control receives the raw binaural waveform. The left- and right-ear waveform samples are flattened and concatenated into a 4608-dimensional feature vector. These amplitudes are supplied as static features to the first learned linear layer, which injects current into the LIF layers. They are not written directly into membrane voltage and are not converted into an externally generated spike train before entering the baseline SNN. This tests whether the localisation task can be learned directly from acoustic samples without an explicit auditory front end.

The second control retains the modelled cochlea but removes the downstream cue pathways. For each ear, the CSR is divided into ten temporal bins and the spikes are counted within each frequency-channel and time-bin pair. The left- and right-ear counts are then flattened and concatenated into a 960-dimensional feature vector. This tests whether the cochlear frequency-time representation alone accounts for the performance benefit, or whether the later cue-specific pathway abstractions add useful structure.

\subsection{Direct and Projected Variants}

Each input-only representation is tested in two forms. The direct variants feed the full raw-waveform or cochlear-raster vector into the trainable SNN. The projected variants first compress the input to 474 dimensions, matching the size of the proposed pathway feature vector, using a fixed Gaussian random projection. The projection is generated once, is not trained, and has no access to the target labels.

The direct variants test whether a larger trainable first layer can learn useful features from minimally processed inputs. The projected variants provide a more size-matched comparison, reducing the possibility that any performance difference is explained only by input dimensionality or parameter count. The combination gives a staged interpretation: a strong raw-waveform baseline would weaken the case for explicit auditory processing; a strong cochlear-raster but weak raw-waveform baseline would support the cochlea while questioning the need for later pathway stages; and stronger proposed-model performance would show that the cue-specific pathway populations provide useful inductive bias.

\section{Summary}

The final model is a hybrid system. The acoustic and auditory pathway stages are fixed, interpretable, and designed around known localisation cues. The SC readout stage converts pathway populations into coordinate estimates using direct COM, calibration, or recurrent attractor dynamics. The residual SNN then learns context-dependent corrections from the full set of pathway features. This structure supports the central claim tested in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}: biologically motivated pathway populations are useful intermediate representations for a small neuromorphic localisation readout.
